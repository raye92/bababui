# 99 Punctuation Rules for Court Reporting — Working Implementation Notes

> Working rule database compiled from user-provided notes/screenshots. This is intended for building an AI transcript QA/correction system, not as a substitute for the original copyrighted rulebook.

## Rule Categories

- **1–17:** Core punctuation and commas
- **18–25:** Semicolons, colons, and speaker labels
- **26–34:** Dashes and question handling
- **35–43:** Quotations
- **44–49:** Parentheticals, italics, ellipses, slashes
- **50–59:** Capitalization
- **60–74:** Numbers, dates, time, money, measurements
- **75–84:** Abbreviations, plurals, possessives, apostrophes
- **85–93:** Prefixes, suffixes, compounds, hyphenation
- **94–99:** Spacing and yes/no answer punctuation
- **100–105:** Transcript structure, paragraphing, and deposition conventions

## Rule 1. Statements, Commands, and Fragments
Use a period to end a statement, command, or independent fragment.

Examples:
- The defendant has an alibi.
- Please speak louder.
- Objection. Compound and vague.

## Rule 2. Common Fragments
Use a period after common fragments such as “okay,” “all right,” “very well,” “fine,” and strong interjections at the beginning or end of a sentence.

Examples:
- All right. What time did you leave?
- It was in the afternoon. Okay.

In deposition transcripts, standalone "Okay." or "All right." on a Q line can also serve as a structural paragraph break before a new topic or before switching to colloquy format. See Rule 105.

## Rule 3. Independent Clauses with Conjunction
Use a comma to separate two independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction, unless both clauses are very short.

Example:
- I went to the grocery store, and I saw a friend from high school.

## Rule 4. Introductory Subordinate Clause or Verbal Phrase
Use a comma after an introductory subordinate clause or introductory verbal phrase.

Examples:
- Before my vacation in Hawaii, I broke my thumb.
- Determined to pass, the student practiced daily.

## Rule 5. Initial Elliptical Clause
Use a comma after an initial elliptical clause that stands for an independent or subordinate clause.

## Rule 6. Introductory Prepositional Phrase
Use a comma after a long introductory prepositional phrase, especially if it contains a verb or clarity requires it.

## Rule 7. Introductory Transitional or Parenthetical Expressions
Use a comma after introductory transitional or parenthetical expressions. One-syllable transitions such as hence, then, thus, so, and yet generally do not take a comma. “Now” and “then” take a comma when they mean “well,” not when they indicate time or sequence.

## Rule 8. Nonessential Clauses and Phrases
Use commas to set off nonessential clauses and phrases.

Examples:
- Pharmco’s main office, which is located in Ohio, has an excellent reputation.
- Keith moved to New York in 2013, when he was twenty-three.

## Rule 9. Appositives
Use commas to set off nonessential appositives or appositive adjectives.

Example:
- I received a letter from Jim, my youngest brother.

## Rule 10. Direct Address
Use commas to separate names or titles used in direct address.

Examples:
- Angela, did you meet your boyfriend for lunch?
- With all due respect, Counsel, I object.

## Rule 11. Interrupting Word, Phrase, or Clause
Use commas to set off a word, phrase, or clause that interrupts the flow of a sentence.

Examples:
- The injured victim, because of the blood loss, collapsed.
- I, you know, worry all the time.

## Rule 12. Introductory Clause or Phrase Before a Second Independent Clause
When an introductory clause or phrase appears before a second independent clause, set it off appropriately with commas or semicolons depending on sentence structure.

Examples:
- We can meet Wednesday afternoon, or if you prefer, we can meet Thursday morning.
- I arrived late to work; and when no one was watching, I slipped into my office.

## Rule 13. Contrasting Phrases
Use commas to set off contrasting phrases.

Common signals: not, rather, rather than, never, but, though, even though.

Examples:
- Plead for mercy, not justice.
- The meetings occurred on Mondays, never Tuesdays.

## Rule 14. Series of Items
Use commas to separate a series of three or more words, phrases, or clauses.

Examples:
- Counsel invited John, Mary, Bob, and Barbara to lunch.
- I bought bread, milk, and eggs.

## Rule 14-1. Series of Items with Repeated Conjunctions
If every item in the series is connected by a conjunction, do not use commas.

Examples:
- Counsel invited John and Mary and Bob and Barbara to lunch.
- I bought bread and milk and eggs.

## Rule 15. Coordinate Adjectives
Use a comma between coordinate adjectives that equally modify the noun.

Example:
- She bought a sleek, shiny car.

Test: If “and” can be inserted between the adjectives, they are likely coordinate.

## Rule 16. Titles and Degrees Following a Personal Name
Use commas to separate titles and degrees following a personal name.

Examples:
- Ruth Walker, CEO, approved the budget.
- Mrs. Anderson, Esq., represented Mr. Smith.

## Rule 16-1. Jr. and Sr. Following a Personal Name
Do not set off Jr. or Sr. with commas unless the person specifically prefers it. Do not use commas with numerical designations.

Examples:
- Martin Luther King Jr.
- John Smith III

## Rule 17. Numbered References
Use commas to separate numbered references to sections, pages, lines, exhibits, and similar items.

Examples:
- Defendant’s Exhibit A, page 4.
- Page 5, line 10.

Capitalize words such as Act, Book, Chapter, Exhibit, Figure, Part, Section, and Volume. Do not capitalize line, note, page, sentence, or verse.

## Rule 18. Use of Semicolons and Dashes
Use semicolons sparingly and dashes only when necessary. Prefer periods over semicolons and semicolons over dashes when possible.

## Rule 19. Independent Clauses Without Conjunction
Use a semicolon to separate two closely related independent clauses that lack a coordinating conjunction.

Example:
- I’m from California; my spouse is from Washington.

A period is also acceptable.

## Rule 20. Joined Independent Clauses with Internal Commas
Use a semicolon when independent clauses are joined by a conjunction and one or both clauses already contain commas.

## Rule 21. Independent Clauses with Transitional Expression
Use a semicolon between independent clauses joined by a conjunctive adverb or transitional phrase.

Examples:
- I heard the scream; however, I did not call the police.
- I took the medicine; as a result, I fell asleep.

## Rule 22. Series of Items with Internal Commas
Use semicolons to separate series items that already contain commas.

Example:
- Santa Cruz, California; Seattle, Washington; and Cincinnati, Ohio.

## Rule 23. Capitalization After Colon
Capitalize the first word after a colon if one or more complete sentences follow.

Example:
- The local laws differ: You are guilty unless proven to be a local resident.

## Rule 24. Independent Clause Followed by Explanation, Series, or Appositive
Use a colon after an independent clause when what follows explains, lists, or renames the first part.

## Rule 25. Speaker Identification and Formatting Modes
Use a colon after speaker identification in transcript formatting.

**Q/A Mode** — Used when the examining attorney questions the witness:

Examples:
- BY MS. MADSEN:
-      Q    What is your name?
-      A    Jordan Smith.

**Colloquy Mode** — Used when speakers address someone other than the Q/A pair, or when non-examining parties speak:

Examples:
- MR. SMITH: Were you at home that evening?
- THE COURT: You may proceed.
- THE WITNESS: I would like to clarify something.

**Switching between modes:** When the examining attorney addresses someone other than the witness, switch from Q/A to colloquy using the attorney's full speaker label. When the witness speaks outside a direct Q/A response, use THE WITNESS. After any colloquy, resume Q/A with a new BY [ATTORNEY]: byline. See Rule 101 for full details.

**Speaker label formatting:**
- Attorney labels: MR./MS./MRS. + SURNAME in caps (e.g., MR. SIMMONS:)
- Judge: THE COURT:
- Witness outside Q/A: THE WITNESS:
- Two spaces after the colon before the text begins.

## Rule 26. Abrupt Changes
Use a dash for abrupt changes, midsentence changes, or speaker interruptions.

Example:
- I got out of my car and jogged -- ran to the overturned sedan.

In deposition Q/A formatting, dashes are also used to show interrupted questions or answers. When a witness answers before the attorney finishes a question, end the first Q fragment with " -- ", show the A response, then continue the Q with " -- " at the start. See Rule 102 for full details and examples.

## Rule 27. Nonessential Elements with Internal Commas
Use dashes instead of commas when setting off a nonessential element that already contains internal commas.

Example:
- We -- Tom, Sue, and Bill -- formed the legal team.

## Rule 28. Introductory Series
Use a dash after an introductory series before an independent clause.

Example:
- Tom, Sue, and Bill -- we formed the legal team.

## Rule 29. Direct Question
Use a question mark after a direct question or elliptical question.

Examples:
- What time did you leave work?
- Were you walking? Jogging? Running?

## Rule 30. Short Midsentence or End-of-Sentence Echo Question
Use commas to separate a short echo question of three words or fewer.

Examples:
- You saw him, didn’t you?
- He was there, right?

## Rule 31. Midsentence Non-Echo Question
Use dashes around a midsentence question that does not echo the sentence or is long.

Example:
- The new document -- do you want to see it? -- will exonerate my client.

## Rule 32. End-of-Sentence Non-Echo Question
Use a period or semicolon before an end-of-sentence question that does not echo the sentence.

Use this especially when the question contains right, correct, or true, or begins with can/do and refers to recollection.

## Rule 33. Questions Following the Verb “To Be”
If a statement ends with a form of “to be” and is followed by a question, use a comma, colon, or no punctuation depending on structure and length. Capitalize the question if separated by comma or colon.

## Rule 34. Series of Connected Questions
Use a question mark after each item in a series of connected or elliptical questions.

Example:
- Were you walking? Jogging? Running?

## Rule 35. Direct Quotations and Capitalization
Use quotation marks around direct quotations. Capitalize the first word if the quotation contains one or more complete sentences.

Example:
- She said, “Amy, not Bill, illegally accessed the checking account.”

## Rule 36. Signal Phrases with Direct Quotations
Separate introductory, interrupting, or concluding signal phrases from quotations with commas.

Common signal verbs: said, asked, responded, stated, replied, yelled, screamed.

## Rule 37. Colon Before Direct Quotation
Use a colon before a direct quotation when an independent clause introduces the quotation or the quotation is more than one sentence.

## Rule 38. “Quote” and “Close Quote”
When spoken punctuation appears, separate quote/open quote/close quote/end quote/unquote with commas and use quotation marks.

## Rule 39. Periods, Commas, Semicolons, and Colons with Quotes
Periods and commas go inside closing quotation marks. Semicolons and colons go outside closing quotation marks.

## Rule 40. Dashes and Question Marks with Quotes
Place a dash or question mark inside quotation marks if it belongs to the quoted material, outside if it belongs only to the surrounding sentence. If it applies to both, place it inside.

## Rule 41. Titles of Chapters, Articles, Songs, Poems, Etc.
Use quotation marks for works contained within larger works, such as articles, chapters, TV episodes, songs, and poems.

## Rule 42. Special Emphasis
Use quotation marks when a word is used unusually, technically, ironically, or in a special sense.

Example:
- What does “final approval” include?

## Rule 43. Definitions and Translations
Use quotation marks around dictionary definitions or translations. Italicize the term being defined or translated.

Example:
- *Vox populi* means “voice of the people.”

## Rule 44. Explanatory Information and Witness Gestures
Enclose explanatory information, witness gestures, and reporter notes in parentheses. Complete-sentence parentheticals should begin with a capital letter and end with a period inside the parenthesis.

Examples:
- (A recess was taken at 1:13 p.m.)
- (The witness marked the exhibit.)

## Rule 45. Legal Citations and Foreign Expressions
Use italics for legal citations, short-form citations, and uncommon foreign expressions. Use v. for versus in legal citations.

Examples:
- *Brown v. Board of Education*
- *res ipsa loquitur*

## Rule 46. Titles of Books, Magazines, Newspapers, Movies, and TV Shows
Use italics for complete works.

Examples:
- *The New York Times*
- *Twilight Zone*

## Rule 47. Omitted Material in a Quotation
Use an ellipsis to indicate omitted words in a quotation. Use three spaced periods with space before and after.

Example:
- “The accident was caused by . . . the defendant.”

## Rule 48. Speaker Trail-Off
Use an ellipsis when a speaker trails off.

Example:
- We were just trying to . . .

Use a dash for interruptions, not an ellipsis.

## Rule 49. Interchangeable Words
Use a slash to show interchangeable or alternate words.

Examples:
- and/or
- quote/unquote
- he/she

## Rule 50. Fundamental Capitalization Rule
Capitalize proper nouns, proper adjectives, and short forms representing the full proper noun.

## Rule 51. First Word of a Sentence
Capitalize the first word after a period or question mark. Do not capitalize after a comma, semicolon, or dash unless another rule requires it.

## Rule 52. Titles and Subtitles
Capitalize the first word, last word, and all major words in titles and subtitles. Do not capitalize articles, short conjunctions, short prepositions, or “to” in infinitives unless first or last.

## Rule 53. Titles with Personal Names
Capitalize a title before a person’s name. Do not capitalize titles following or replacing a name, except for high-ranking governmental titles and family titles in direct address.

Examples:
- President Lincoln
- Abraham Lincoln, president
- Thank you, Mom.

## Rule 54. North, South, East, and West
Capitalize directional words only when referring to recognized geographical regions.

Examples:
- the South
- drive south

## Rule 55. Political Sections and Nicknames
Capitalize recognized political regions, nicknames, or geographic nicknames.

Examples:
- Bay Area
- Big Apple
- Down Under

## Rule 56. Organizations, Businesses, and Government Bodies
Capitalize the full official names of organizations, businesses, schools, universities, government agencies, committees, and boards. Generally do not capitalize short forms unless they represent national or international governmental bodies.

## Rule 56-1. Names of Courts
Capitalize the name of a specific court. Capitalize Court when referring to the Supreme Court or the presiding judge acting as the Court.

## Rule 57. Brand, Trade, and Product Names
Capitalize brand, trade, and product names. Do not capitalize trademarks that have become generic common nouns.

Examples:
- Tylenol
- Ford
- zipper

## Rule 58. Days, Months, Holidays, Seasons, Historic Events
Capitalize days, months, holidays, and historic events. Do not capitalize seasons or academic seasons.

Examples:
- Wednesday
- March
- Christmas
- Revolutionary War
- summer

## Rule 59. Races, Peoples, Languages, and Religion
Capitalize peoples, ethnic groups, languages, religions, and major religious references.

Examples:
- Native American
- Mandarin Chinese
- Sikhism
- Bible

## Rule 60. Spelled-Out Numbers vs. Figures
Spell out numbers one through ten. Use figures for numbers above ten and for dates, time, addresses, money, technical measurements, percentages, fractions in certain cases, decimals, numbered references, and ages.

## Rule 60-1. Expressing Numbers in Words
Hyphenate compound numbers from twenty-one through ninety-nine.

Example:
- twenty-one

## Rule 60-2. Writing Numbers with Figures
Use commas for thousands, millions, and larger numbers. Do not use commas in years, page numbers, addresses, or ZIP codes.

Examples:
- 1,250
- 2025
- 9112 Main Street

## Rule 61. Numbers Above and Below Ten in the Same Context
When related numbers above and below ten appear in the same context, use figures for both.

Example:
- I own 12 chickens and 3 dogs.

## Rule 62. Millions, Billions, and Trillions
Use figures plus the words million, billion, or trillion.

Examples:
- 1.3 million
- $22 trillion

## Rule 63. Numbers at the Beginning of a Sentence
Spell out a number at the beginning of a sentence unless it must remain a figure, such as an account number or identifier.

## Rule 64. Dates
Use figures for dates. Month-year and month-day formats do not take commas. Numeric dates are acceptable where appropriate. Foreign or military style places the day before the month.

Examples:
- July 2016
- April 5
- 10/15/2025
- 17 June 2018

**Spoken ordinals in dates:** When a witness speaks an ordinal date ("July 1st," "the 5th," "March 23rd"), preserve the ordinal in the transcript. Do not strip ordinals to cardinal numbers. Court reporters record what was said. See also Rule 104.

## Rule 65. Time
Use figures with a.m. and p.m. Use lowercase a.m. and p.m.

Examples:
- 7 a.m.
- 8:15 p.m.
- 1900

## Rule 66. O’Clock
Spell out the number before o’clock.

Examples:
- five o’clock
- eight o’clock

## Rule 67. Money
Use figures for money. Drop trailing .00 when appropriate. Use consistent style for amounts under one dollar.

Examples:
- $500
- 89 cents
- $.89

## Rule 68. Addresses
Use figures for street numbers and ZIP codes.

Example:
- 220 Cornwall Road, Woodside, California 94062

## Rule 69. Technical Measurements
Use figures for measurements and spell out units.

Examples:
- 7 acres
- 3 pounds
- 5 feet 8 inches

## Rule 70. Percentages
Use figures plus the word percent.

Examples:
- 97 percent
- 12.5 percent

## Rule 71. Fractions
Spell out and hyphenate simple fractions. Use figures for long fractions, mixed numbers, and technical measurements.

Examples:
- one-half
- two-thirds
- 17/32
- 3 1/2 inches

## Rule 72. Decimals
Use figures for decimals. Use a leading zero before the decimal point when necessary, except with gun caliber.

Examples:
- 2.3 pounds
- 0.3 grams
- .45 caliber

## Rule 73. Numbered References
Use figures for numbered references. Abbreviate number as No. and numbers as Nos. when it comes between the reference word and the figure.

Examples:
- Exhibit 4
- Page 50, line 17
- Questionnaire No. 39
- Policy No. 2304997

## Rule 74. Ordinals
Spell out ordinals from first through tenth. Use figures for ordinals over tenth.

Examples:
- first
- tenth
- 12th

## Rule 75. Plural of a Figure
Form the plural of a figure by adding s.

Examples:
- 1980s
- 50s

**Standalone decade references:** When a decade is referenced without the century (e.g., "the 70s," "the 80s"), no apostrophe is needed. Write "the 70s" not "the '70s." The apostrophe form ('70s) is only appropriate when the context specifically emphasizes the omission of "19" and that shortened form was spoken.

## Rule 76. General Principles of Abbreviation
Check a Merriam-Webster dictionary for proper abbreviation usage. Capitalized abbreviations generally have no spaces or periods. Lowercase abbreviations generally have periods and no spaces.

## Rule 76-1. Professional Degrees or Titles After Personal Name
Use periods in professional degrees or titles that follow a personal name.

Examples:
- M.D.
- D.D.S.

## Rule 77. Abbreviate Only These Words
Do not use abbreviations unless spoken, except Mr., Mrs., Ms., and Dr. before a personal name and Jr. and Sr. after a personal name. Do not abbreviate okay or et cetera.

## Rule 78. Plural of an Abbreviation
Form the plural of a capitalized abbreviation by adding s. Form the plural of a lowercase abbreviation by adding apostrophe-s.

Examples:
- TVs
- IOUs
- bcc’s

## Rule 79. Singular Possessive
For most singular nouns, add apostrophe-s. For singular nouns ending in s or z, add an apostrophe or apostrophe-s depending on what is heard.

Examples:
- defendant’s testimony
- Reynolds’ car
- Reynolds’s car

**Academic degrees:** Always use the possessive apostrophe in academic degree names. These are possessive forms (a degree "of a bachelor," a degree "of a master").

- bachelor’s degree / bachelor’s of science / bachelor’s of arts
- master’s degree / master’s of science / master’s of arts
- associate’s degree

Do NOT capitalize these as formal titles (e.g., do NOT write "Bachelor’s of Science" or "Bachelor of Science") unless the witness spoke the full formal name of a specific program. Preserve the witness’s phrasing but always add the apostrophe. See Rule 104.

Note: Doctorate, M.D., Ph.D., and J.D. do not take an apostrophe — they are not possessive forms.

## Rule 80. Plural Possessive
For plural nouns ending in s, add an apostrophe. For plural nouns not ending in s, add apostrophe-s.

Examples:
- investors’ goals
- children’s toys

## Rule 81. Possessive or Descriptive?
If the phrase can be rewritten with “of,” “of the,” or “of a,” use an apostrophe. Otherwise, treat it as descriptive.

Examples:
- girls’ softball team
- leaders guide

## Rule 82. Plural of a Lowercase Letter or Likely Misread Word
Use apostrophe-s for the plural of a lowercase letter or any word likely to be misread.

Examples:
- p’s and q’s
- two a’s

## Rule 83. Omission of Letters or Figures
Use an apostrophe to show omitted letters or figures.

Examples:
- ’91
- rock ’n’ roll
- 2015/16

## Rule 84. To Create a Verb Form
Use an apostrophe before the suffix of a letter, number, or abbreviation to form a verb.

Examples:
- x’d
- x’ing
- 69’d

## Rule 85. Common Prefixes and Suffixes
Common prefixes and suffixes are added to form a solid compound word.

Examples:
- midblock
- postmortem
- agreeable
- statewide

## Rule 86. Prefix to Capitalized Word, Number, or Word That Can Be Misread
Use a hyphen to join prefixes to a capitalized word, a number, or a word that can be misread.

Examples:
- mid-June
- anti-American

**Common court-reporting prefix words — preferred forms:**
- postoperative (solid, Rule 85)
- preoperative (solid, Rule 85)
- nonpreserved (solid, Rule 85)
- pre-subject (hyphenated — could be misread as "presubject")
- pre-existing (hyphenated by convention in legal/medical contexts)
- cross-talk (hyphenated, per Merriam-Webster)
- cross-communication (hyphenated)
- rehab-based (hyphenated compound adjective, Rule 91)

## Rule 87. Full-Time, Part-Time, Uh-Huh, Uh-Uh, and X-Ray
Use Merriam-Webster spellings: full-time, part-time, uh-huh, uh-uh, and X-ray for noun/adjective. X-ray or x-ray may be used as a verb depending on style.

## Rule 88. Suspending Hyphens
Use a suspending hyphen at the end of each adjective, prefix, or suffix when it applies to a following or preceding hyphenated word.

Examples:
- 12- and 24-month terms
- 9-, 11-, and 14-year-old children
- pre- and post-op anxiety

## Rule 89. Spelled-Out Words
Use hyphens between letters of spelled-out words.

Examples:
- C-y-n-d-i
- M-c D-o-u-g-a-l-l

## Rule 90. General Principles of Compound Words
Check Merriam-Webster to determine whether a compound noun, adjective, or verb is open, hyphenated, or closed. If uncertain, best practice is to hyphenate.

Examples:
- strong-armed
- test-drive
- downloaded

## Rule 91. Determining a Compound Adjective
If words before a noun work together as a unit to express one idea, they form a compound adjective.

Examples:
- well-known actor
- secondhand smoke

## Rule 92. Common Compound Adjective Forms
Do not hyphenate compounds formed by an -ly adverb plus an adjective or verb. Many other compound adjectives before a noun are often hyphenated, including noun + adjective, noun + participle, adjective + participle, adjective + noun ending in -ed, and number + noun.

Examples:
- poorly written sentence
- newly formed business
- fat-free milk
- home-cooked meal
- high-ranking official
- deep-rooted fear

## Rule 93. Long Compound Adjective
If a hyphenated compound adjective is six or more words or could be misread, enclose it in quotation marks.

Examples:
- His “I’m going out of my freaking mind” smile scared me.
- She gave me a “you can’t make me do it” expression.

## Rule 94. Period and Question Mark Spacing
Use no space before and one space after a period or question mark. With monospaced/typewriter-like fonts, two spaces after a period or question mark may be used.

Examples:
- Objection. Compound. Vague.
- Did you have a good lunch? I hope so.

## Rule 95. Comma, Semicolon, and Colon Spacing
Use no space before and one space after a comma, semicolon, or colon. Exceptions: no space after a comma in numbers and no space after a colon in time. With monospaced fonts, two spaces after a colon may be used.

Examples:
- Before you mail the package, stop at the bank and withdraw $1,000.
- The children arrived at 9:30 a.m.; the bus had a flat tire.

## Rule 96. Hyphen, Slash, and Dash Spacing
Use no spaces before or after a hyphen or slash. For an em dash, use no spaces before or after the dash. For a dash made of two hyphens, use one space before and after the dash.

Examples:
- win-win
- quote/unquote
- the affected nations—the U.S., Canada, and Mexico—signed the agreement.
- the affected nations -- the U.S., Canada, and Mexico -- signed the agreement.

## Rule 97. Quotation Marks and Parentheses Spacing
Use one space before and no space after an opening quotation mark or parenthesis. Use no space before and one space after a closing quotation mark or parenthesis. Exception: no space before a question mark after a closing quotation mark.

Examples:
- Did he really say, “I love you,” after the first date?
- Did he really say, “I love you”?
- (The witness marked Exhibit C.)

## Rule 98. Yes/No Confirms Statement
Use a comma to separate yes or no if it echoes or confirms the previous statement/question. The yes or no is parenthetical in this case.

Examples:
- Yes, I do.
- I do, yes.
- No, I didn’t see her leave.

## Rule 99. Yes/No Explains or Adds Information
Use a period after yes or no if the following statement explains or adds information.

Examples:
- No. I work for the county.
- Yes. I work under Sergeant Lewis.
- No. I was in a meeting until 6:00 p.m.

## Rule 100. Paragraph Breaks Within Testimony

Insert a new paragraph (indented continuation line) within a Q or A block when any of the following conditions are met:

1. The speaker shifts to a new topic, subtopic, or time period.
2. The speaker uses a transitional phrase that introduces a new thought (e.g., "So," "Now," "In terms of," "And then," "I mean," "I will add," "The other thing is," "There's a third item").
3. The speaker returns from a digression or parenthetical aside.
4. The speaker begins a summary or conclusion after a detailed explanation.
5. The response exceeds approximately 8–10 lines of transcript text without a break.

A new paragraph within a Q or A block is formatted as an indented continuation line (10 spaces of indentation), not a new Q or A label.

Do NOT insert a paragraph break:

- In the middle of a single flowing sentence.
- Between every sentence in a short answer (3–4 lines).
- After every transitional word — only when the word marks a genuine shift in topic or structure.

Examples:

WRONG (no paragraph breaks in long answer):
```
     A    That's a great question, and it's a
complicated answer, which is, it's nuanced.  So if I'm
doing a QME, or if I'm the PTP, either environment, if a
patient comes in and they have a full thickness complete
tear of their rotator cuff, it's pretty obvious that
they need surgery.  You don't even need to be a physician
to figure that out.  Now, the flip side of that though is
sometimes you'll get patients who have tendinosis.
Those cases are gray zones.  So, again, the answer is
nuanced.  It really depends on the nature of the story.
```

RIGHT (paragraph breaks at each major shift):
```
     A    That's a great question, and it's a
complicated answer, which is, it's nuanced.  So if I'm
doing a QME, or if I'm the PTP, either environment, if a
patient comes in and they have a full thickness complete
tear of their rotator cuff, it's pretty obvious that
they need surgery.  You don't even need to be a physician
to figure that out.
          Now, the flip side of that though is sometimes
you'll get patients who have tendinosis.  Those cases
are gray zones.
          So, again, the answer is nuanced.  It really
depends on the nature of the story.
```

Common transitional triggers for paragraph breaks:
- "So," / "So, again," / "So, for example,"
- "Now," / "Now, the flip side"
- "In terms of" / "As far as"
- "I will add" / "I would just add"
- "The other thing is" / "There's a third item"
- "But" / "But, yes,"
- "I mean," (when introducing a new thought, not as filler)
- Shift from general principle to specific example
- Shift from one body part, injury, or topic to another
- Return from a digression to the main point
- Shift from past to present tense or one time period to another

This rule applies to BOTH Q and A blocks. Long questions from attorneys also need paragraph breaks at topic shifts.

## Rule 101. Colloquy vs. Q/A Mode Switching

In deposition transcripts, there are two formatting modes:

**Q/A Mode:** Used when the examining attorney questions the witness.
```
     Q    What is your name?
     A    Jordan Smith.
```

**Colloquy Mode:** Used when attorneys address each other, the court, or the reporter — or when the witness speaks outside of a direct Q/A response.
```
     MS. MADSEN:  Counsel, do you stipulate?
     MR. SIMMONS:  Yes.
```

### When to switch from Q/A to Colloquy:

Switch to colloquy format whenever the examining attorney addresses someone other than the witness (opposing counsel, the court, the reporter, or the room generally). Use the attorney's full speaker label (e.g., MS. MADSEN:) instead of Q.

When the witness speaks during a colloquy exchange (not in response to a direct question), use THE WITNESS as the speaker label instead of A.

### When to switch from Colloquy back to Q/A:

After any colloquy exchange, insert a BY [ATTORNEY NAME]: byline before resuming Q/A examination.

### Examples:

WRONG:
```
     Q    Okay.  Counsel, do you stipulate to waive the
admonitions?
     MR. SIMMONS:  I'm fine to waive.
```

RIGHT:
```
     Q    Okay.
     MS. MADSEN:  Counsel, do you stipulate to waive the
admonitions?
     THE WITNESS:  I'm fine to waive.
```

The key test: If the Q line is addressed to someone other than the witness, it must be reformatted as a colloquy line with the attorney's speaker label.

## Rule 102. Interrupted Q/A Formatting (Dash Continuation)

When a witness answers before the examining attorney finishes a question, or when the attorney interrupts the witness:

1. End the first part of the interrupted line with " -- " (space-hyphen-hyphen-space).
2. Show the interrupting speaker's response as a normal Q or A line.
3. Continue the interrupted speaker's line with " -- " at the beginning, lowercase (unless another capitalization rule requires uppercase).

This convention shows the reader that the second fragment is a continuation of the first, not a new independent question or answer.

Examples:

WRONG (looks like two separate questions):
```
     Q    So your QME work is focused on the pain
management aspect?
     A    Correct.
     Q    As well as anesthesiology?
```

RIGHT (shows the Q was interrupted):
```
     Q    So your QME work is focused on the pain
management aspect --
     A    Correct.
     Q    -- as well as anesthesiology?
```

### How to identify an interruption:

- The second Q or A fragment does not stand alone as a complete thought.
- The second fragment begins with a lowercase word, a conjunction, or a continuation phrase (e.g., "as well as," "and then," "but").
- The first fragment ends mid-thought without natural closure.

If both halves are complete independent thoughts, they are NOT an interruption — format them as separate Q/A lines.

## Rule 103. Transcript Section Headers

Each examination section of a deposition transcript must begin with a centered header identifying the type of examination. This header appears on its own line above the "BY [ATTORNEY NAME]:" byline.

Required headers:
- EXAMINATION
- CROSS-EXAMINATION
- REDIRECT EXAMINATION
- RECROSS-EXAMINATION
- FURTHER EXAMINATION
- FURTHER CROSS-EXAMINATION

Example:
```
                       EXAMINATION
BY MS. MADSEN:
     Q    Good morning...
```

The header should be centered (approximately 23 spaces of indentation for standard transcript formatting).

## Rule 104. Preserving the Witness's Spoken Phrasing

Do not normalize, correct, or rewrite a witness's spoken phrasing of proper nouns, degree names, titles, place names, or technical terms beyond adding standard punctuation and capitalization.

The court reporter records what was said, not what should have been said.

Specifically:

- If a witness says "bachelors of science," write "bachelor's of science" (add the apostrophe per Rule 79, maintain the phrasing). Do NOT change to "Bachelor of Science."
- If a witness says "July 1st," preserve "July 1st." Do NOT strip the ordinal to "July 1."
- If a witness says "workers' comp," preserve "workers' comp." Do NOT expand to "workers' compensation" unless the full phrase was spoken.
- If a witness says a name differently than expected, preserve what was said.

The test: Would this change alter what the reader believes the witness said? If yes, do not make the change.

## Rule 105. Standalone Fragment Paragraph Breaks

When "Okay" or "All right" appears at the beginning of a Q line followed by a shift to a new topic or a question directed to someone else, separate it onto its own Q line.

Example:

WRONG:
```
     Q    Okay.  Counsel, do you stipulate to waive?
```

RIGHT:
```
     Q    Okay.
     MS. MADSEN:  Counsel, do you stipulate to waive?
```

WRONG:
```
     Q    All right.  Now, bringing attention to where
we are currently...
```

RIGHT:
```
     Q    All right.
          Now, bringing attention to where we are
currently...
```

This rule interacts with Rule 101 (Colloquy Switching). When "Okay" precedes a colloquy-mode statement, separate the "Okay" as its own Q line, then switch to colloquy format for the next line.

When "Okay" precedes a continuation of the same topic to the same listener, it does NOT need to be separated:
```
     Q    Okay.  And your QME license, what specialty
is it in?
```
(This is fine — "Okay" leads directly into the next question to the same witness on the same topic.)
